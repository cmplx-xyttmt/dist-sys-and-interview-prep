Publishing the ladder

Every rung page ends with a "Write about it" section naming its post. This page holds the parts that belong to the series as a whole: the title, the introductory post, the post plan, and what would change if the ladder itself went public. Section 8 of the dist-sys roadmap sets the two rules that apply throughout: interactive explorations rather than plain prose where a mechanism is central, and at most sixty minutes of polish per post so writing never displaces the prep.

What to call it

You asked for something broader than Sprites, since the Sprites design is the capstone and a public version would swap some content. Three candidates, with the case for each:

  • From the disk up (recommended). Names what the ladder does: it starts at blocks on a disk and climbs to a cloud product. It says nothing about Sprites, so the capstone can change. It reads as a series title and as a site section. Subtitle for the series page: the systems knowledge underneath one cloud product, one rung at a time.
  • Ladder to a blog post. Names the method instead of the content: pick a post you cannot read, build the prerequisites as a ladder, climb, re-read. This is the reusable idea (the heuristic-contests guide is a second ladder), and it fits your introduction's opening sentence exactly. Weaker as a title for a reader who has not read the introduction, so better as the name of the site section that holds several ladders, with "From the disk up" as this ladder's title inside it.
  • Computers for agents, from scratch. Tied to the capstone; strongest hook for a Fly.io or agents-infra reader, weakest if the capstone changes. Keep it for the capstone post's own title.

Recommendation: the site gets a section called Ladders; this one is From the disk up; the introduction is titled How I read a blog post I couldn't understand; the capstone is Computers for agents, from scratch: re-reading the Sprites post.

The introduction, post 0

Your draft, kept in your words, with two additions marked: the ladder's shape so readers know what they are signing up for, and the honest line about the checkpoints, which is what makes the series different from a reading list.

I often come across technical blog posts that present an interesting-seeming idea, but I can't quite understand the details. It's usually because of a knowledge gap, by virtue of not having spent time working in that particular technology. I've always wanted to learn more about distributed systems and systems programming in general, but often didn't know where to start, or felt overwhelmed by the sheer amount of things I didn't know.

I recently came across such a blog post: The design and implementation of Sprites, Fly.io's account of building a Linux computer that starts in a second, keeps its disk forever, and sleeps for free. I read it and understood very little of it. So I decided to use it as a starting point. My goal was to understand the design of Sprites, and along the way learn about containerisation, filesystems, virtualisation, object storage, replication and gossip: the things the post assumed I already knew.

Addition 1, the shape:

The method was a ladder. I listed everything the post assumed, put the items in the order each one needs the one before, and gave each rung a small program to write with a check it had to pass. Nine rungs, one a week, about three hours each. The last rung is re-reading the post.

Addition 2, the honest line:

Each post in this series is one rung: what I had to learn, the small program, the number it produced, and the thing that surprised me. I'm writing them as I go, so the early ones are written by someone who does not yet understand the later ones. That's the point. If you can't read the Sprites post either, the ladder is here, and you can climb it beside me.

Two suggestions for the draft itself. Cut "e.t.c." and name the topics (done above); a reader trusts a list more than an etcetera. And put the Sprites post's own one-line promise in the first paragraph, so a reader who has never heard of Sprites knows why the design is worth eight weeks.

If the ladder itself goes public

The pages work from file:// with no server, so the folder can be copied into the Hugo site's static/ladders/from-the-disk-up/ and served as is; progress stays in each reader's own browser. Before that, five changes:

  1. Strip what is yours. Season dates, WEEK.md, the Tuesday and Thursday slots, "Isaac", the roadmap links, the showcase's Tunga references. A reader has their own calendar; the guide should say "one rung a week" and stop.
  2. Make the capstone swappable. The explainer page is Sprites-specific and stays as this ladder's top. But the index should say the method plainly: pick a post, list what it assumes, order the list, give each item a mission. A second ladder (the heuristic-contests guide already exists) makes the section credible.
  3. Keep the checkpoints as they are. Labeled evidence fields and pass conditions are what separate this from a reading list. Add an "export your progress" note and nothing else.
  4. Own the readings' framing. Some "why" notes say "you own the book"; a public version says "chapter 3 of DDIA, 60 minutes" and offers a free alternative where one exists (the SQLite posts and OSTEP chapters already are).
  5. Rename the files. sprites-guide/ becomes from-the-disk-up/; rung slugs stay. Add a licence line (CC BY for the text, MIT for the scripts) and a "found a mistake" link.

Not before the season ends. Publishing the ladder is a post 8 decision; publishing the posts starts at post 0, this week, in the sixty minutes the roadmap allows.
